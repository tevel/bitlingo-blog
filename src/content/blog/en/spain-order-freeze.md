You freeze **after** your own sentence, when the waiter, driver, or pharmacist answers and you have no next line. A phrase list gets you to “Un café con leche, por favor.” It does not get you through “¿Para tomar aquí o para llevar?”

The fix is a **20-minute audio drill** of twelve prompt-and-reply pairs, said out loud before you land in Madrid, Barcelona, Seville, Valencia, Granada, or San Sebastián.

## Key facts for a trip to Spain

- Speak Spanish in Barcelona. Read six Catalan signs so the exit and the opening hours are obvious.
- **La carta** is the full menu. **El menú del día** is the set-price lunch.
- Dinner service often starts around 20:30 or 21:00. Ask what time they open.
- Pay the amount on the bill. Rounding up, or leaving the small change, is the usual tip.
- The emergency number in Spain is **112**.
- In Madrid, **z** and **c** before **e** or **i** often sound like the **th** in *think*. *Gracias* sounds like GRAH-thyahs. In much of Andalusia and the Canary Islands, that sound is an **s**. Both understand you.

## What the freeze actually is

You prepared the opener. The local person prepared a routine question, because they ask it a hundred times a day. The silence you feel is the gap between those two sentences.

That gap shows up in the same places:

- A café, when the next word decides whether the cup stays or goes in a bag.
- A taxi, when the price arrives as one mumbled number.
- A hotel desk, when they ask for the passport you already have in your hand.
- A pharmacy, when you need a simple pain reliever and the instructions come in a string of hours.

BitLingo is built for this gap. You load the lines you will say and the lines you will hear, then listen and repeat with the phone in a pocket. The [offline practice guide](/blog/en/offline-practice-guide/) is how that deck survives airplane mode on the way to Spain.

## La carta and el menú del día

This mix-up creates a real freeze on day one.

- **La carta, por favor.** You want the full list of dishes.
- **El menú del día, por favor.** You want the set lunch, usually two courses, a drink, and a lower price. It is the ordinary weekday lunch in Spain.
- **¿Qué recomienda?** You want them to pick.

If you say only “el menú,” a busy waiter may hand you the set lunch or ask which one you mean. Ask for the thing you want by its name.

## Madrid, Barcelona, and the rest of Spain

### Madrid

Staff speak fast, and **vale** (BAH-leh) pops up as “OK.” Your Spanish still works. The useful extra line is the dinner clock: many kitchens that looked closed at 18:30 open later.

**You say:** ¿A qué hora abren para cenar? (ah keh OH-rah AH-bren PAH-rah seh-NAR)
**You hear:** A las nueve. / A partir de las ocho y media.

### Barcelona

Signs, street names, and station boards are often in **Catalan**. People serving food and hotel desks in the tourist center answer in Spanish, and often in English. Speak Spanish. Use Catalan as a reading skill, so a closed door does not feel like a language failure.

Answer **Bon dia** with **Buenos días**. That is a normal exchange.

### Seville, Valencia, Granada, San Sebastián

Use the same spoken deck. In Andalusia the consonants get lighter and **c/z** often sound like **s**. In the Basque Country and Galicia, official signs are bilingual. The strategy matches Barcelona: read the sign, speak Spanish.

## The twelve replies

Respellings follow central Spain, where **z** sounds like **th**. Say each line out loud. The recording in your deck matters more than the spelling.

### 1. Coffee

**You say:** Buenos días. Un café con leche, por favor. (BWEH-nohs DEE-ahs. oon kah-FEH kon LEH-cheh, por fah-VOR.)
**You hear:** ¿Para tomar aquí o para llevar? (PAH-rah toh-MAR ah-KEE oh PAH-rah yeh-VAR.)
**You answer:** Para tomar aquí. / Para llevar.

### 2. A table

**You say:** Una mesa para dos, por favor.
**You hear:** ¿Tienen reserva? / ¿Dentro o en la terraza?
**You answer:** No, no tenemos. En la terraza, por favor.

### 3. The menu

**You say:** La carta, por favor.
**You hear:** ¿Quieren ver el menú del día?
**You answer:** Sí, el menú del día. / La carta, gracias.

### 4. An allergy

Say this every time you order, including when the dish looks safe. Use **alérgico** if you are male and **alérgica** if you are female. Swap in **gluten** or **marisco** (shellfish) when that is the allergy.

**You say:** Soy alérgico a los frutos secos. ¿Este plato lleva frutos secos?
**You hear:** No, no lleva. / Sí, lleva.
**You answer:** Entonces ese, por favor. / ¿Qué tiene sin frutos secos?

### 5. The bill

**You say:** La cuenta, por favor. (lah KWEHN-tah)
**You hear:** ¿Juntos o por separado? / ¿Con tarjeta?
**You answer:** Juntos, con tarjeta.

Leave coins or round up if you want to tip. A speech about 20 percent belongs to a different country.

### 6. A taxi

**You say:** ¿Cuánto cuesta hasta esta dirección?
**You hear:** Unos quince euros. ¿Efectivo o tarjeta? (eh-fek-TEE-voh oh tar-HEH-tah)
**You answer:** Con tarjeta, por favor.

Show the address on your phone while you ask. **Quince** is 15. **Quince con cincuenta** is 15.50.

### 7. The hotel

**You say:** Tengo una reserva a nombre de… (TEN-goh OO-nah reh-SEHR-bah ah NOM-breh deh)
**You hear:** ¿Me deja el pasaporte?
**You answer:** Sí, aquí tiene.

### 8. Directions

**You say:** ¿Dónde está la estación de metro?
**You hear:** Todo recto y a la derecha. (TOH-doh REK-toh ee ah lah deh-REH-chah)
**You answer:** Muchas gracias.

**A la izquierda** is “to the left” (ah lah eeth-KYEHR-dah).

### 9. The pharmacy

This is for a common pain reliever you already know you can take. For an emergency, call **112**.

**You say:** Me duele la cabeza. ¿Tiene paracetamol? (meh DWEH-leh lah kah-BEH-thah)
**You hear:** Sí. Tome una cada ocho horas.
**You answer:** Perfecto, gracias.

### 10. Too fast

**You say:** ¿Puede repetir más despacio, por favor? (PWEH-deh reh-peh-TEER mahs des-PAH-thyoh)
**You hear:** They repeat, or they ask ¿Habla inglés?
**You answer:** Un poco. Más despacio, por favor.

### 11. Water

**You say:** Una botella de agua, por favor.
**You hear:** ¿Con gas o sin gas?
**You answer:** Sin gas, por favor.

### 12. Dinner time

**You say:** ¿A qué hora abren para cenar?
**You hear:** A las nueve. / A partir de las ocho y media.
**You answer:** Vale, gracias.

## Prices you will hear

## The 20-minute drill

Twelve pairs, about ninety seconds each, is one sitting.

1. Build a BitLingo deck with two cards per situation: the line you say, then the line you will hear. The [custom vocabulary workflow](/blog/en/custom-vocabulary-workflow/) is the short way to do that.
2. While you still have a signal, open the deck so the audio is on the phone. Use the [offline guide](/blog/en/offline-practice-guide/) before the flight, not in the jet bridge.
3. On the plane, listen and repeat each pair twice. Say the answer out loud.
4. From the airport to the hotel, play the replies with the phone in your bag. The [commute guide](/blog/en/commute-hands-free/) is the same hands-free pattern.
5. The next morning, one pass of only the lines you will hear.

A ten-minute pass fits the [habit playbook](/blog/en/habit-playbook-bitlingo/) if you want the deck still fresh on day three.

## If you already studied Spanish

Classroom Spanish still leaves this hole. You can conjugate *querer* and still miss **¿Para tomar aquí o para llevar?** because nobody drilled the reply at full speed. Treat the deck as ear practice for Spain, including **vale**, **la carta**, and the Catalan signs. Your old vocabulary stays useful the moment the next sentence is familiar.

## FAQ

### Do I need to speak Catalan in Barcelona?

No. Order and ask for help in Spanish. Learn six Catalan signs — Sortida, Gràcies, Bon dia, Si us plau, Obert, and Tancat — so you can read the exit and the door. Answer Bon dia with Buenos días.

### How many Spanish phrases do I need before a week in Spain?

Twelve pairs are enough. Each pair is the line you say and the line you will hear back, covering coffee, a table, the menu, an allergy, the bill, a taxi, the hotel, directions, a pharmacy, asking someone to slow down, water, and dinner time. Twenty minutes of listen-and-repeat before you land is the whole drill.

### What do I say when the Spanish answer is too fast?

Say ¿Puede repetir más despacio, por favor? Then wait. If they offer English, you can still repeat your Spanish line slowly. The drill is what makes the second hearing understandable.

### Does the same Spanish work in Madrid, Seville, Valencia, and Granada?

Yes for speaking. People in Madrid, Seville, Valencia, Granada, and San Sebastián understand this deck. The pronunciation of c and z changes by region. In Barcelona, the Basque Country, and Galicia, signs may use another language. Speak Spanish anyway.

### What is the difference between la carta and el menú in Spain?

La carta is the full menu. El menú del día is the set-price lunch. Ask for la carta when you want the list of dishes, and for el menú del día when you want the cheaper lunch.

### Can I practice these Spanish replies without looking at my phone?

Yes. Put the twelve pairs in a BitLingo deck and play them as audio. Repeat out loud on the flight. In the taxi, keep the phone in your bag and listen hands-free.
